ARGUMENT

“Stalking consists of two elements: (1) the respondent ‘must intentionally or knowingly engage in a course of conduct directed at’ the petitioner and (2) the respondent ‘must know or [act with recklessness regarding] the course of conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear for the person’s own safety or suffer other emotional distress.” Ragsdale v. Fishler, 2025 UT App 36, ¶ 31, 567 P.3d 586 (“Ragsdale II”) (quoting Ragsdale v. Fishler, 2021 UT 29, ¶ 25, 491 P.3d 835 (“Ragsdale I”)); see also Counterman v. Colorado, 600 U.S. 66, 80, 143 S.Ct. 2106[FN 1] “[E]motional distress is defined as ‘significant mental or psychological suffering.’” Ragsdale II, 2025 UT App 36, ¶ 31 (quoting Utah Code Ann. § 76-5-106.5(1)(a)(ii)(A)). “Both elements must be present before a district court may issue a civil stalking injunction.” Id. 

Here, Matthias cannot prove either element to justify the Requested Injunction. Further, the Utah Court of Appeals recently reiterated that the First Amendment cabins the courts’ ability to issue a civil stalking injunction to only specific “parameters.” See id. ¶ 46. Content-based 

[FN 1] Counterman is instructive to the instant case for many reasons. Foremost, it prompted a change in the Stalking Statute’s definition of stalking such that a petitioner must show at least “recklessness” with respect to the effects of the course of conduct.

restrictions are “presumptively unconstitutional” and subject to “strict scrutiny.” See id. ¶ 47 (citation omitted). The Requested Injunction seeks content-based restrictions on speech and cannot withstand strict scrutiny. The Requested Injunction is unconstitutional.

A. Matthias Cannot Show She Is Entitled to the Requested Injunction under the Stalking Statute.

A civil stalking injunction requires the petition to prove stalking as defined in Utah Code 76-5-106.5 (“Section 76-5-106.5”). That section provides that “an actor commits stalking if the actor intentionally or knowingly: (a) engages in a course of conduct directed at a specific individual and knows or is reckless as to whether the course of conduct would cause a reasonable person: (i) to fear for the individual’s own safety or the safety of a third individual; or (ii) to suffer other emotional distress.” See Section 76-5-106.5(2). A “course of conduct” requires “two or more acts directed at or toward a specific individual.” See Section 76-5-106.5(1). “[T]he burden is on the petitioner to show by a preponderance of the evidence that stalking of the petition by the respondent has occurred.” See Utah Code Ann. § 78B-7-701(5)(b)(ii).

1.  Matthias Fails to Prove a Course of Conduct.

Matthias’s Declarations recite only two instances where Christina M communicated “specifically” with her. And in both instances, Matthias initiated the conversation. First, at the Maricopa County Courthouse, Matthias approached and greeted Christina M. Immediately after, Matthias insisted that Christina M participate in a lengthy conversation about Matthias’s past statements and conduct. Later in the month, Matthias again initiated contact with Christina M this time by text, to make accusations and demands upon Christina M. As before, Christina M reluctantly participated in the exchange. Christina M could not have known that the communications were unwanted because Matthias initiated them. Indeed, Christina M made no attempt to communicate

Page 6
specifically with Matthias directly or indirectly after the April text where Matthias stated she did not wish to be contacted.

Because Matthias cannot point to specific communications directed at Matthias, she attempts to rely on many unrelated and irrelevant statements by others, others’ conduct, and Christina M's online posts and public statements. The problem is that none of these satisfy the definition of “course of conduct” because none of them are “directed at or toward” Matthias specifically. See Section 76-5-106.5(1)(a)(i).

Statements and conduct by Lindzie H, Janeese S, and Lori D. The Matthias Declarations and Yohr Declaration spend considerable time detailing statements and conduct of Linzie H, Janeese S, and Lori D. Lindzie H, Janeese S, and Lori D refute Matthias and Yohr’s allegations in their declarations. But, even if the allegations are true (they are not), what is noticeably absent is any proof that Christina M engaged in or influenced any of these events. In some instances, she learned about them afterwards and discussed them publicly later. That is not enough to show course of conduct.

Online posts and public comments. The Stalking Statute is clear that an act or statement does not meet the definition of “course of conduct” unless it is directed at a “specific individual." See id. Matthias asks the Court to parse the contents of Christina M's online posts and infer that Christina M intended Matthias to see them. Such an exercise is impermissible under the Stalking Statute and problematic under the First Amendment as discussed below. And, even if it were appropriate to extrapolate Christina M intent from the content of her posts, the posts that Matthias references show that Christina M is not attempting to address specifically Matthias, her family, or Yohr directly or indirectly. The posts are clearly public commentary directed at the

Page 7
public generally. Matthias fails to prove “course of conduct” as the first required element for the Requested Injunction. 

2. Matthias Cannot Show that the Reasonable Person in Her Circumstances Would Experience Fear or Emotional Distress. 

Matthias must also show that Christina M knew or was reckless “as to whether the course of conduct would cause a reasonable person: (i) to fear for the individual’s own safety or the safety of a third individual; or (ii) to suffer other emotional distress.” See Section 76-5-106.5. “The second element of the stalking inquiry imposes ‘an objective standard under which the subjective effect of the respondent’s conduct on the petition is irrelevant.’”  Ragsdale II, 2025 UT App 36, ¶ 33 (quoting Ragsdale I, 2021 UT 29, ¶ 45). “‘Courts must avoid succumbing to a purely subjective analysis, which is inconsistent with the objective standard’s intent to protect against criminalizing conduct that only an unreasonably sensitive or paranoid [petitioner] would find harassing.’” Id. (quoting Baird v. Baird, 2014 UT 08, ¶ 27, 322 P.3d 728) (alteration in original). “‘Courts applying thing individualized objective standard have considered such factors as the [petitioner’s] background, the [petitioner’s] knowledge of and relationship with the [respondent], and history of abuse between the parties, . . . and the cumulative effect of [the respondent’s] repetitive conduct.’” Id. (alterations in original) (quoting Baird, 2014 UT 08, ¶ 27). The foregoing factors, enumerated in Baird and later in Ragsdale are not an exhaustive list, and courts may examine other relevant factors. See id. 

Here, Matthias cannot show that Christina M knew or acted recklessly in such a way that the reasonable person in Matthias’s circumstances would experience fear or emotional distress. Matthias is a public figure who continually exercises her right to speak publicly about her life, her friends, and a variety of other topics.  Knowing this, Christina M had no reason to expect that 

Page 8
Matthias would attempt to quash Christina M's speech—much less through two false police reports and this proceeding.

Further, the Baird factors all weigh strongly against a finding that a reasonable person under these circumstances would experience fear or emotional distress. As discussed above, Matthias’s background as a public figure, “ex-reporter,” and social commentator certainly subjects her to public scrutiny. It simply is not reasonable that Matthias would expect to continually engage in public discussion, including negative statements about individuals, without encountering speech she disagrees with and does not like.

Similarly, the only instances Matthias identifies where Christina M communicated with her directly or indirectly related to negative statements and conduct directed toward Christina M and others. Matthias has no reasonable basis to expect that a friend or former friend would not discuss interpersonal issues with her—especially where, as here, Matthias initiated the communication every time. Matthias attempts to turn her falling out with Christina M into an injunction with real implications for Christina M's civil rights. That is not the purpose of the Stalking Statute.

The cumulative effect of Christina M's alleged conduct also does not warrant the Requested Injunction. As discussed above, with no real “course of conduct” to support her request, Matthias unleashes a torrent of unrelated allegations about others’ conduct and Christina Ms protected First Amendment activity. All of that does not “count” under the Stalking Statute, Baird, and other cases. The degree of culpability required prevents the Court from “counting” events that Christina M learned about only later. For example, Matthias has no good faith basis to claim that Christina M intended or acted recklessly with respect to incidents in Boise when Christina M was in Europe and learned about only after the fact.

A number of other factors not specifically enumerated in Baird also weigh strongly against the Requested Injunction. First, Matthias already submitted two police reports with the same sort of misrepresentations currently before Court. In each instance, the police did not substantiate Matthias’s claims.

Second, nearly all of the statements and conduct that Matthias references occurred in public places or online.  Matthias claims that they interfered with her “work,” but Christina M, Lindzie H and others also work in the true crime space or have been regular attendees of trials, press conferences, and conventions. Lindzie H, Christina M, Janeese S, and Lori D have just as much right to be in the public places and participate in public events as Matthias. This is not a situation where Christina M has accosted Matthias at home or any other non-public space. 

Third, Matthias’s own conduct cuts strongly against any notion that she feared for her safety.  Matthias and Yohr repeatedly approached, confronted, or attempted to confront Christina M, Lindzie H, Janeese S, and others. Not only does this contradict Matthias’s testimony that she subjectively feared these individuals or was distressed, but a reasonable person in her circumstances would not engage in the conduct that Matthias has engaged in.

Finally, the Matthias Declarations and the Yohr Declaration, on their face, deal only with Matthias’s alleged subjective feelings. The Baird court specifically warned against granting an injunction based solely on subjective factors. See Baird, 2014 UT 08, ¶ 27. Relatedly, Matthias makes no attempt to connect the dots between Christina M's alleged statements and conduct and the extreme subjective reaction Matthias alleges. In sum, Matthias fails to show requisite mental state, causation, or objectively reasonable fear or emotional distress.

Page 10
B. The Requested Injunction Is Unconstitutional. 

Earlier this year, the Utah Court of Appeals in Ragsdale II reiterated the framework for determining whether a requested injunction violates the First Amendment. See 2025 UT App 36, ¶¶ 45–65. “In assessing whether a civil stalking injunction violates the respondent’s free speech rights, district courts must, at a minimum, determine whether each provision of a proposed injunction is content-based or content-neutral, and evaluate each provision under the corresponding level of scrutiny.” Id. ¶ 46. Content-restrictions are “‘subject to strict scrutiny regardless of the government’s benign motive, content-neutral justification, or lack of animus toward the ideas contained in the regulated speech.’” Id. ¶ 47 (quoting Reed v. Town of Gilbert, 576 U.S. 155, 164, 135 S. Ct. 2218 (2015). “Such restrictions overcome strict scrutiny only if ‘they are narrowly tailored to serve compelling state interests.’” Id. (citation omitted). “In the strict-scrutiny context, a restriction is narrowly tailored if it is ‘the least restrictive means to further the articulated interest.’” Id. (quoting Sable Communications of Cal., Inc. v. F.C.C., 492 U.S. 115, 109 S.Ct. 2829).

A content-based restriction is one that “‘stifles speech on account of its message.’” Id. (quoting Turner Broad. System, Inc. v. F.C.C., 512 U.S. 622, 641, 114 S.Ct. 2445).  “In other words, a restriction is content-based ‘if it require[s] enforcement authorities to examine the content of the message that is conveyed to determine whether a violation has occurred’ or if it is ‘concerned with undesirable effects that arise from the direct impact of speech on its audience or listeners’ reactions to speech.’” Id. (quoting McCullen v. Coakley, 573 U.S. 464, 479, 134 S.Ct. 2518). “This ‘extends not only to a restriction on a particular viewpoint, but also to a prohibition of public discussion of an entire topic.’” Id. (quoting Burson v. Freeman, 504 U.S. 191, 197, 112 S.Ct. 1846). “Additionally, restrictions that appear content-neutral on their face

Page 11
are treated as content-based if they ‘cannot be justified without reference to the content of the  regulated speech’ or if they ‘were adopted by the government because of disagreement with the message the speech conveys.’” Id. (quoting Reed, 576 U.S. 155, 164, 135 S.Ct. 2218). 

In this case, each provision of the Requested Injunction is content-based and does not withstand strict scrutiny.

Personal Conduct – This section requests an order commanding Christina M to “not stalk” Matthias and then recites and references the statutory definition of stalking.  This provision is problematic for three main reasons. First, as a practical matter, it is vague, unenforceable, and redundant of other provisions. Utah law already prohibits stalking, and there would be no way to enforce the order without resort to the Courts. The parties are attending the Hearing to determine if stalking has occurred. There is no reason to enter an order that requires the Court to do the exact same thing any time Matthias believes Christina M is stalking her. This is especially true since it is not clear what the Personal Conduct Order adds apart from the other provisions.

Second, the issuance of the Requested Injunction in any form would impose on Christina M's civil rights and violate the First Amendment because Matthias relies on Christina M's protected speech to establish stalking. Basically, Matthias is asking the Court to agree with Matthias’s public speech and disagree with Christina M's. Such a restriction would be content-based because it is justified only with resort to Christina M's message with the Court adopting Matthias’s viewpoint, punishing Christina M and, in essence, removing an entire topic (Matthias’s own statements and conduct) from public discussion. There is no compelling government interest in favoring one speaker over another and a broad (so broad as to be meaningless) restriction on “stalking” is far from being narrowly tailored enough to withstand strict scrutiny.

Page 12
Third, the Personal Conduct Order in the Requested Injunction mirrors almost exactly the one the Utah Court of Appeals reviewed in Ragsdale II. The Ragsdale II court held that the provisions purporting to bar conduct that would “annoy, harass, or cause distress” to the petitioner were content-based and could not withstand strict scrutiny. 2025 UT App 36, ¶¶ 58–59. The court reasoned that “[t]he additional prohibition against annoying or causing distress could potentially enjoin [respondent] from talking about [petitioner] to others.” Id. ¶ 59. The court was concerned that, for example, “any appearance that [respondent] might make before the local governing authorities to complain about [the presence of petitioner’s business in the neighborhood] could likely cause [petitioner] emotional distress or would, at the very least, annoy her.” Id.

No Contact – Matthias asks the Court to bar Christina M from communicating directly or indirectly with Matthias and others. But this section must also be read in conjunction with the “Other Assistance Needed” section where Matthias asks the Court to order Christina M to “cease and desist any and all online activity and communications (direct or indirect) relating to or referencing [Matthias].” Again, this restriction is content based because it purports to quash speech on an entire topic. See Ragsdale II, 2025 UT App 36, ¶ 47.

In this way, the No Contact order in the Requested Injunction is distinguishable from the no contact order upheld in Ragsdale II and Towner v. Ridgeway, 2008 UT 23, 182 P.3d. The upshot of both those cases is that a stalking injunction that prohibits communicating with or to a person may be permissible where a prohibition on communicating about a person is not (or at least must withstand strict scrutiny). See Ragsdale II, 2025 UT App 36. ¶ 53. The Ragsdale II court summarized that Towner held that “the civil stalking injunction did not violate the First Amendment because it enjoined the respondent from communicating directly to the petitioner 

Page 13
but did not enjoin him from speaking about the petitioner.” Id. ¶ 59. The court further noted that in Ragsdale II and in Towner the no contact provisions “prohibited any communication, either direct or indirect, from the respondent to the petition or other identified individuals . . . . As with the provisions at issue in Towner, nothing in the No Contact Order precluded [respondent] from expressing his opinions—political or otherwise—about the presence of [petitioner] in his neighborhood to others not identified by the No Contact Order.” See id.

Stay Away Order – Matthias asks for an order that Christina M stay away from Matthias’s home, work, school, and vehicle. But, as with the No Contact Order, Matthias modifies the forms language and requests an order that Christina M must stay away from “Online Forums and other Social Media Accounts (e.g. TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, Reddit, etc.).”  This extra requirement is problematic on multiple levels. First, the restriction is content-based and cannot withstand strict scrutiny. On its face, the restriction appears to ban Christina M from the Internet. In one swath, Matthias hopes the Court will eliminate Christina M's ability to frequent all online public spaces. This “ask” alone unmasks Matthias’s goal of eliminating Christina M's ability to speak out on issues of public interest. The ban would be akin to allowing Matthias a popular pulpit in a public park while prohibiting others who disagree with her from the same public spaces. The First Amendment squarely forecloses this sort of preference. 

Second, to the extent that Matthias’s request is meant to be interpreted more narrowly, it is unconstitutionally vague because it does not put Christina M on notice of the extent of her ban. See Greenwood v. City of North Salt Lake, 817 P.2d 816, 819–820 (Utah 1991) (holding that a restriction is unconstitutionally vague where it does not provide adequate notice of what the restriction covers). 

Page 14
In sum, the imposition of the Requested Injunction in any form would violate Christina M's First Amendment rights because Matthias relies upon protected free speech to try to establish the elements of stalking. As such, any restriction is content-based because the Court can find the necessary elements only by examining the contents of the speech and by preferring Matthias’s speech over Christina M's. Similarly, each proposed provision of the Requested Injunction fails to pass First Amendment muster. The Requested Injunction is unconstitutional. 

CONCLUSION 

Matthias is allowed to not like what Christina M has said about her. But Matthias falls far short of the statutory elements required under the Stalking Statute. And Christina M right to free speech forecloses the Requested Injunction. Christina M respectfully requests that the Court deny the Requested Injunction.

LEGAL ANALYSIS

I. RESPONDENT’S “PUBLIC FIGURE” DEFENSE HAS NO BASIS IN UTAH STALKING LAW

A.  The Stalking Statute Makes No Distinction Based on Public Profile

Respondent’s central argument—that Petitioner’s social media presence somehow diminishes her protection from stalking—represents a fundamental misunderstanding of Utah law. Utah Code § 78B-7-701 et seq. protects “any person” from stalking behavior. The statute contains no exception, limitation, or modification based on a person’s public visibility, social media following, or participation in public discourse.

Respondent incorrectly imports the “public figure” doctrine from defamation law, where it affects the burden of proof under New York Times v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964). This doctrine has no application to stalking law. See Bauer v. State, 511 P.3d 1016, 1023 (Nev. 2022) (“The heightened standard for public figures in defamation cases does not extend to criminal harassment or stalking prosecutions”); People v. Relerford, 104 N.E.3d 341, 349 (Ill. 2017) (“A victim’s status as a public or private figure is irrelevant to stalking analysis”).

B.  Public Visibility Does Not Waive Protection from Targeted Harassment

Respondent argues that because Petitioner “uses these platforms to speak out on a range of issues,” she should expect and tolerate the alleged stalking behavior. This argument conflates two distinct legal concepts:

Page 2

1. Protected public discourse and criticism (First Amendment protected)
2. Targeted harassment and stalking (criminal/tortious conduct)

The First Amendment protects robust public debate and criticism. It does NOT protect:

- Courses of conduct intended to cause fear or emotional distress
- Targeted harassment campaigns
- True threats
- Stalking behavior

See Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003); Counterman v. Colorado, 600 U.S. 66, 74 (2023) (“The First Amendment permits restrictions on speech that threaten unlawful violence or consist of targeted harassment”).

II. RESPONDENT’S SOCIAL MEDIA ACCOUNTS CONSTITUTE TARGETED HARASSMENT, NOT PUBLIC DISCOURSE

A.  The Respondent’s Social Media Accounts Exist Solely to Attack Petitioner

The most compelling evidence that Respondent’s conduct constitutes stalking rather than protected speech is the singular purpose of her social media accounts. Respondent has created at least two separate social media accounts (TikTok and Instagram) dedicated exclusively to disparaging Petitioner under the thin veil of discussing an “ex-friend.”

This is not a case where Respondent maintains general social media accounts that occasionally reference Petitioner while discussing various topics. These accounts were created for one purpose only: to conduct a sustained campaign attacking Petitioner’s moral character. When 100% of an account’s content targets a single individual, it cannot be characterized as public discourse—it is a harassment campaign.

B.  The “Ex-Friend” Identifier Proves Personal Targeting

- A public official’s policies
- A business’s practices
- Issues affecting the community
- Matters of legitimate public interest

Instead, this is precisely what it appears to be: a personal vendetta arising from a failed friendship, conducted through social media platforms. The term “ex-friend” itself demonstrates that Respondent’s grievances are personal, not public.

C.  Utah’s Stalking Statute Explicitly Covers Communications “About” an Individual

Utah Code § 76-5-106.5(1)(a)(i) is unambiguous in its scope. The statute defines “course of conduct” to include “acts in which the actor... communicates to or about an individual... (I) directly, indirectly, or through any third party; and (II) by any action, method, device, or means.” (emphasis added).

This statutory language is critical to this case. The Legislature deliberately included communications “about” an individual, not just “to” them. This means that Respondent cannot escape liability by claiming she never directly contacted Petitioner. Creating social media accounts that communicate about Petitioner to third parties—through 50+ posts and videos over several months—falls squarely within the statute’s plain language. 

The Legislature’s inclusion of “or about” recognizes the reality of modern harassment: stalkers often attempt to harm their victims through indirect means, including social media

Page 4

campaigns designed to damage reputation, recruit others to their cause, or create public humiliation. Respondent’s TikTok and Instagram accounts, dedicated exclusively to posting content about her “ex-friend,” constitute precisely the type of indirect harassment the statute prohibits.

Furthermore, the statute’s language “by any action, method, device, or means” explicitly contemplates modern technology. Social media platforms are a “device” and “means” of communication. When someone creates multiple accounts across different platforms for the sole purpose of posting content about one individual over several months, they have engaged in a course of conduct under the plain statutory language.

D.  No Legitimate Public Interest Exists

The Supreme Court has long distinguished between speech on matters of public concern and purely private disputes. See Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443 (2011). Personal grievances between former friends do not constitute matters of public concern that warrant First Amendment protection when used as weapons for harassment.

Respondent cannot transform a private vendetta into protected public discourse simply by posting it online. Unlike legitimate public interest speech such as:

- Consumer warnings about dangerous products
- Exposing public corruption
- Discussing community safety issues
- Political or policy commentary

Respondent’s campaign serves no public interest. It is revenge masquerading as free speech.

III.  APPLYING THE RAGSDALE FRAMEWORK TO SOCIAL MEDIA HARASSMENT

A.  Utah’s Statute Specifically Includes Communications “About” Individuals

Page 5

Before applying Ragsdale, it is crucial to understand the statutory framework. Utah Code  § 76-5-106.5(1)(a)(i) defines “course of conduct” as including acts where the actor “communicates to or about an individual...directly, indirectly, or through any third party.” This “or about” language is dispositive here.

Respondent cannot claim First Amendment protection or argue she never “contacted” Petitioner when the statute explicitly prohibits communications about an individual. The Legislature’s choice to include “or about” demonstrates clear intent to capture indirect harassment campaigns—exactly what social media enables. When Respondent created accounts posting 50+ times about her “ex-friend” over several months, she communicated “about” Petitioner to third parties, satisfying the statutory element.

This interpretation aligns with the statute’s purpose: preventing stalkers from circumventing the law through indirect means. Without the “or about” language, stalkers could freely conduct harassment campaigns through social media, claiming immunity because they never directly contacted their victim. The Legislature foreclosed this loophole.

B.  The “Directed At” Standard in Digital Contexts

Ragsdale v. Fishler, 2025 UT App 36, provides the controlling framework. The court distinguished between speaking ABOUT someone (potentially protected) and directing conduct AT someone to cause distress (stalking). Critically, the Ragsdale court found stalking even when the defendant claimed to be protesting only a business, not targeting the owner personally.

Here, Respondent’s conduct is even more clearly “directed at” Petitioner:

- The accounts exist solely to discuss Petitioner

Page 6

- All content references Petitioner directly or through transparent “ex-friend” references
- The posts are designed to reach Petitioner’s social circle
- The clear intent is to damage Petitioner’s reputation and cause emotional distress

C.  Course of Conduct Through Multiple Platforms

Creating multiple social media accounts to target one person satisfies the “course of conduct” requirement under Utah Code § 76-5-106.5. Each account creation, each post, and each attempt to expand the audience for these attacks constitutes a separate act in the stalking pattern. The use of multiple platforms (TikTok and Instagram) demonstrates escalation and obsessive behavior, not legitimate commentary.

Here, the numbers speak for themselves: 50+ posts and videos about Petitioner over a six-month period across multiple platforms. This far exceeds the statutory minimum of “two or more acts.” Each of these 50+ posts represents a deliberate choice to continue the harassment campaign. This is not a case of one or two ill-considered posts in a moment of anger—this is a sustained, deliberate campaign spanning half a year. The sheer volume and duration demonstrate the obsessive nature of Respondent’s conduct and her intent to cause ongoing distress.

D.  The Cumulative Effect of Social Media Campaigns

The Ragsdale court emphasized examining the “cumulative effect” of conduct. A social media harassment campaign creates unique cumulative harm:

- Permanent digital footprint of attacks
- Viral potential expanding the audience
- 24/7 accessibility of harassing content
- Recruitment of others to participate in harassment

- Ongoing nature creating fear of escalation

IV. RESPONDENT MISAPPLIES THE “REASONABLE PERSON” STANDARD

A.  The Standard Does Not Change Based on Public Profile

Respondent’s Opposition states: “It simply is not reasonable that Matthias would expect to continually engage in public discussion... without encountering speech she disagrees with.” This fundamentally misunderstands the reasonable person analysis under Utah Code § 76-5-106.5. 

The proper inquiry is whether a reasonable person in Petitioner’s circumstances would experience fear or emotional distress from the specific conduct alleged—not whether public figures should generally expect criticism. The statute asks whether the respondent’s “course of conduct would cause a reasonable person... to fear for the individual’s own safety... or to suffer other emotional distress.” Utah Code § 76-5-106.5(2).

B.  Social Media Harassment Creates Unique Reasonable Fear and Emotional Distress

A reasonable person would experience significant distress from:

- Multiple accounts dedicated to attacking their character
- Persistent posting about personal matters
- Public humiliation before their social and professional circles
- The permanence of online content
- The potential for escalation from online to physical harassment

Accepting Respondent’s interpretation would mean:

- Anyone with social media forfeits protection from stalking
- Online harassment is categorically protected
- Digital stalking receives greater protection than physical stalking

- Victims must choose between online presence and personal safety

V. RESPONDENT’S CONSTITUTIONAL ARGUMENTS FAIL

A.  Anti-Stalking Laws Are Constitutional Even When Applied to Speech

Respondent suggests that any restriction on speech about Petitioner violates the First Amendment. This is incorrect. Courts nationwide have upheld stalking statutes and injunctions against First Amendment challenges. See State v. Pauling, 69 P.3d 331, 336 (Wash. 2003) (“Anti-stalking statutes are constitutional restrictions on speech and conduct”). 

The Supreme Court in Counterman v. Colorado recently reaffirmed that states may constitutionally prohibit true threats and stalking conduct, requiring only a showing of recklessness regarding the threatening nature of communications. 600 U.S. at 79-80.

B.  Strict Scrutiny Analysis Does Not Forbid the Requested Injunction

Even if strict scrutiny applies here, which it does not if this is targeted harassment, properly tailored stalking injunctions remain constitutional because:

1.  Compelling Government Interest: Protecting citizens from fear, targeted harassment, and stalking is a compelling interest. Ragsdale v. Fishler, 2025 UT App 36, ¶ 47.
2.  Narrow Tailoring: The Requested Injunction seeks only to prevent stalking conduct—not legitimate criticism or protected speech. It is narrowly tailored to serve the government’s interest in protecting Petitioner from targeted harassment online. 

It is ultimately up to the Court to decide on the final language of the injunction. Should the Court determine the proposed language is too vague or overbroad, the solution is not to discard it entirely as the Respondent argues. Rather, the Court should simply tailor and narrow the language it finds too restrictive. Whatever language the Court deems appropriate, the Respondent cannot be allowed

to transform targeted harassment into protected speech simply by claiming First Amendment protection.

CONCLUSION

Respondent’s Opposition rests on two fundamental legal errors: (1) the belief that public visibility diminishes one’s right to be free from stalking, and (2) the claim that social media accounts dedicated to attacking an “ex-friend” constitute protected public discourse. Both positions find no support in Utah law and would create dangerous precedents. 

The statutory language is clear and dispositive: Utah Code § 76-5-106.5(1)(a)(i) explicitly prohibits communications “to or about an individual.” The Legislature did not limit the statute to direct contact—it deliberately included “or about” to capture indirect harassment campaigns. Respondent’s creation of multiple social media accounts posting 50+ times about Petitioner over six months falls squarely within this statutory prohibition. Each of those 50+ posts represents a deliberate choice to continue communicating “about” Petitioner to third parties, far exceeding the statutory minimum of two acts required for a course of conduct.

Utah’s civil stalking statute protects all persons equally, regardless of their public profile or social media presence. The statute’s inclusion of communications “about” individuals demonstrates the Legislature’s clear intent to prevent exactly what Respondent has done here: conduct an indirect harassment campaign through social media. To hold otherwise would eviscerate stalking protections in the modern digital age and create an untenable legal precedent whereby anyone with an online presence forfeits basic personal safety protections.

The evidence clearly establishes that Respondent has engaged in a sustained campaign of

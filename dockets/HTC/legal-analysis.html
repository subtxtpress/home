Overview

Lauren Matthias, petitioner, filed for a civil stalking injunction against Christina, respondent, in August 2025. The court issued a temporary ex parte order on Sept 4, 2025. At the first hearing on Sept 22, 2025, the judge narrowed the order from the bench, explicitly rejected the social media ban as a First Amendment violation, and continued the hearing to Oct 16, 2025 to allow full testimony. On the morning of Oct 14, 2025, the court denied petitioner's motion to close the hearing and approved media coverage. That same afternoon, petitioner's counsel moved to dismiss. The case was closed without any ruling on the merits and without petitioner ever testifying under oath or being cross-examined.

One year later, respondent petitioned to expunge the temporary order under Utah Code § 78B-7-1003. Petitioner filed an opposition on Oct 26, 2026, continuing to characterize respondent as "the stalker" and citing respondent's public video content as evidence of ongoing harm. This analysis identifies six categories of legal error in petitioner's filings and assesses what remedies remain available to the respondent.

Key finding The petitioner obtained a one-sided temporary order, voluntarily dismissed before that order could be tested at a full evidentiary hearing, and is now using the procedural gap she created to block expungement. The record supports arguments for expungement, fee awards under the stalking statute, and URCP Rule 11 sanctions.

Procedural History

  • Aug 27, 2025: Petitioner files declaration and request for civil stalking injunction. Declaration signed "by Counsel with permission via email."
  • Sept 4, 2025: Court issues temporary civil stalking injunction ex parte, without notice to respondent. The pre-printed order states "there is reason to believe stalking has occurred" and "the Respondent is the stalker." No evidence heard, no cross-examination.
  • Sept 17, 2025: Petitioner files ex parte motion to enforce the injunction and for sanctions, alleging respondent violated the order via social media posts.
  • Sept 22, 2025: First hearing. Judge Wright calls the temporary order "a one-sided order that was initially signed that was based on one-sided information." Two of respondent's witnesses (Janeese S. and Lindzie H.) are sworn in and testify under oath. Judge narrows the order from the bench, explicitly rejecting the social media ban as a freedom of speech issue. Hearing continued to Oct 16, 2025.
  • Oct 10, 2025: Petitioner moves to continue the Oct 16th hearing.
  • Oct 14, 2025: Morning: Judge Wright denies petitioner's motion to continue, denies motion to close the hearing, and approves Law & Crime's request to cover the proceedings. Afternoon: Petitioner's counsel moves to dismiss. Court grants dismissal. Case closed.
  • September 2026: Respondent petitions to expunge record under Utah Code § 78B-7-1003.
  • Oct 26, 2026: Petitioner files opposition to expungement. Continues to label respondent "the stalker" and cites respondent's public videos as evidence of ongoing harm warranting denial.
Critical pattern Petitioner dismissed her own case two days before a hearing she had moved to close and that a media outlet had been approved to cover. The timing strongly suggests strategic retreat from anticipated adverse rulings rather than good-faith resolution.

Issue 1: False or Materially Misleading Statements in the Initial Declaration

High Severity Rule 11 Relevance Criminal Penalty Exposure

The Boise Restaurant Incident

Petitioner's Aug 27, 2025 declaration (¶¶ 38–50) describes the Boise restaurant confrontation as a coordinated surveillance and stalking operation by "Christina's group of friends." She claims the group waited outside for her to leave, and that police took a stalking report.

The record directly undercuts this narrative on three points:

  • Christina was not present. Lindzie H. testified under oath at the Sept 22, 2025 hearing that Christina was in Europe at the time. Petitioner's declaration heavily implies Christina orchestrated the Boise encounter but never discloses that Christina was not there. The respondent's opposition brief (Sept 17, 2025) states this expressly: "Matthias has no good faith basis to claim that Christina intended or acted recklessly with respect to incidents in Boise when Christina was in Europe and learned about only after the fact."
  • The police investigation found no validity to petitioner's claims. The Henderson Police Department body camera footage released via public records request, and referenced in respondent's YouTube video (Jul 24, 2026), reflects that the investigating officer's report found no proof of hacking, extortion, or threats. The report description states: "the investigation did not find any validity to Lauren Matthias's claims of extortion, hacking, and noted there was no proof from a direct source that text messages had been shared by a specific person."
  • Petitioner's framing reverses the sequence of events. Declaration ¶ 48 states "we had no idea that this group had eaten dinner at the same restaurant that evening." Lindzie H.'s hearing testimony establishes that her group was at the restaurant first, and left after. The waiter corroborated this sequence. Petitioner framed herself as the target of surveillance when the evidence indicates the opposite: her group arrived second and then called police.

The declaration was signed under Utah Code § 78B-18a-106, which subjects declarants to criminal penalty for false statements submitted to the court. Petitioner's counsel noted the signature was given "by Counsel with permission via email," an authentication mechanism that raises its own issues (see Issue 6 below).

The Brother / Sperm Allegation (¶¶ 22–26)

Petitioner's declaration devotes considerable space to a claim that respondent spread a narrative that petitioner "wanted to non-consensually collect my brother's sperm." This is framed as proof of a malicious campaign. However, petitioner does not provide the underlying source material respondent allegedly discussed, and the respondent's filings indicate this was petitioner's own statement or implication in a different context that respondent's followers then commented on. The declaration uses the emotional weight of this claim to characterize respondent as malicious without establishing that respondent fabricated the underlying information. A declaration submitted under criminal penalty that uses inflammatory framing to substitute for factual substance is an evidentiary problem under Rule 11.

Issue 2: "No Evidentiary Hearing" Claim Is Contradicted by the Record

High Severity Direct Misrepresentation to Court

Petitioner's Oct 26, 2026 expungement opposition states: "No evidentiary hearing on the request was held, and the Court made no determination on the merits."

This is factually wrong. The Sept 22, 2025 hearing transcript establishes:

  • Two witnesses (Janeese S. and Lindzie H.) were sworn in before the court.
  • Both testified under oath and were subject to direct and cross-examination.
  • The judge presided, ruled on evidentiary objections (sustaining and overruling multiple objections from both sides), and issued bench rulings narrowing the injunction.
  • Respondent's exhibits 11–13 were presented, and the court considered their admissibility.

Whether the Sept 22, 2025 proceedings satisfy the specific statutory language of § 78B-7-1003(5)(a)(i) ("dismissed, dissolved, or expired upon a hearing by the court") is a legal question the court must resolve. But petitioner's assertion that "no evidentiary hearing was held" is a factual misrepresentation. The court conducted evidentiary proceedings, swore in witnesses, and issued substantive bench rulings. Petitioner's counsel was present throughout.

Misrepresentation risk Asserting to the same judge who conducted the Sept 22, 2025 hearing that "no evidentiary hearing was held" is a significant credibility problem. Judge Wright presided over those proceedings personally. Respondent's counsel should draw this directly to the court's attention.

Issue 3: Ex Parte Order Was Overbroad and Unconstitutional on Its Face

Medium Severity First Amendment Ragsdale II

The original petition included a request that respondent "cease and desist any and all online activity and communications relating to or referencing Petitioner." The judge at the 09-22-2025 hearing explicitly addressed this:

"This civil stalking injunction was in no way meant to silence what Miss Christina is doing. And I want to be very clear on that. And any permanent stalking injunction will not be for that purpose. That is not what a stalking injunction is for."

The Utah Court of Appeals' 2025 decision in Ragsdale v. Fishler, 2025 UT App 36 ("Ragsdale II"), decided earlier that same year, was directly on point. That decision held that provisions barring conduct that would "annoy, harass, or cause distress" were content-based restrictions subject to strict scrutiny and could not withstand it. A blanket ban on all online speech "relating to or referencing" the petitioner is an even broader content-based restriction. Petitioner's counsel filed this request after Ragsdale II was published. Under URCP Rule 11, counsel is required to conduct a reasonable inquiry into whether a legal position is supported by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument for its extension. The requested online speech ban was neither.

The judge's bench ruling on Sept 22, 2025 clarified that the order did not cover social media posts and narrowed it to direct threats and defamation. This means the three alleged violations in the sanctions motion (see Issue 4) were based on conduct that the court itself said was never covered by the order.

Issue 4: The Sanctions Motion Was Based on Conduct the Court Later Said Was Protected

Medium Severity Rule 11 Relevance

On Sept 17, 2025, petitioner filed an ex parte motion to enforce the temporary stalking injunction and for sanctions. The motion alleged respondent violated the injunction through social media posts. Five days later, at the Sept 22, 2025 hearing:

  • The judge stated the order was never intended to cover social media.
  • The judge explicitly said the online speech restriction raised "freedom of speech issues."
  • The judge amended the order to narrow it to threats and defamation, consistent with Ragsdale II.
  • The judge told petitioner's counsel directly: "This civil stalking injunction was in no way meant to silence what Miss Christina is doing."

A motion for sanctions premised on violations of an order provision the court immediately disclaimed was, at minimum, an overreach. Three of the six alleged violations cited in that motion were social media posts. Under URCP Rule 11(b)(2), counsel certifies that claims are "warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument" for its modification. The blanket social media ban was legally untenable under Ragsdale II, which petitioner's counsel knew or should have known.

Issue 5: Expungement Opposition Relies on Protected Speech as Evidence of Ongoing Harm

High Severity First Amendment Equitable Issues

Petitioner's Oct 26, 2026 expungement opposition identifies three post-dismissal events as evidence that respondent "has continued her conduct directed at Petitioner" warranting denial of expungement:

CrimeCon (May 29, 2026)

Petitioner's declaration describes respondent filming petitioner at a public convention with a tripod camera. Respondent was directed to move and complied. She then filmed from a different location. Both parties were at the same public event, a true crime convention. Being present at the same public event and filming public activity is not stalking under Utah law. The judge's Sept 22, 2025 bench ruling addressed precisely this: "It does sound like she has a right to be there and she's got a purpose for being there." The "as far apart as reasonably possible" standard he articulated applies to passive public presence, not an absolute prohibition.

YouTube Statement About Dr. John Matthias (Aug 5, 2026)

Petitioner cites respondent's statement that Dr. Matthias "might have his license, but he is a fraud." She presents this as harassment. However:

  • Respondent's Exhibit 2 in the expungement opposition clarifies what respondent actually said: that Dr. Matthias holds a PhD in "Education (Counseling Psychology)" rather than a "PhD in Psychology" as the Matthias family has claimed.
  • Dr. Matthias's own declaration (¶ 1) states he holds "a Doctorate in Counseling Psychology from the University of Southern California." This aligns with respondent's characterization, not petitioner's.
  • A statement of opinion about a public figure's professional qualifications, grounded in publicly verifiable credential information, is substantially true and therefore not actionable defamation. Petitioner is inadvertently documenting that the respondent's factual premise was accurate.

YouTube Video Description (Jul 24, 2026)

Petitioner's Exhibit 5 is a video description containing the phrases "lauren matthias is the stalker" and "lauren matthias stalking lies." This is public commentary about a matter of court record, specifically the litigation petitioner herself initiated. Under Towner v. Ridgeway, 2008 UT 23, and Ragsdale II, speech about a person directed at the public is First Amendment protected. The judge was explicit about this at the Sept 22, 2025 hearing.

The self-defeating argument Petitioner argues that expungement should be denied because respondent continues to talk publicly about the case. The judge said the order "was in no way meant to silence" respondent. Using that same speech now as grounds to keep a "stalker" label on respondent's record, through an order petitioner never proved, is precisely the speech suppression the judge rejected. The expungement statute's "may" language gives the court discretion; equitable principles strongly favor granting expungement where petitioner created the procedural gap by dismissing before the hearing.

Statutory Construction Problem

Petitioner argues under § 78B-7-1003(5)(a)(i) that the order cannot be expunged because it was not "dismissed, dissolved, or expired upon a hearing by the court." This argument is technically correct as a matter of statutory text. But petitioner manufactured that procedural posture herself: she voluntarily dismissed on the afternoon of Oct 14, 2025, after losing a motion to close the hearing and after a media outlet was approved to cover it. Courts have inherent equitable authority to prevent a party from benefiting from a procedural gap she created. The "may" language in the statute preserves that discretion. Respondent should argue this directly.

Issue 6: Procedural Irregularities in Declarations

Lower Severity Authentication

Multiple petitioner declarations are signed "by Counsel with permission via email" or "signed with permission by email." This applies to Lauren Matthias's Aug 27, 2025 declaration (signed under § 78B-18a-106), the Oct 26, 2026 expungement declaration, and others.

Under Utah Code and the verification requirements of the civil stalking statute, declarations under criminal penalty are personal to the declarant. Counsel signing on behalf of a client "with email permission" creates a chain-of-custody problem: there is no sworn statement from the declarant that the signature was authorized at the time it was affixed, nor a notarized acknowledgment. If any factual assertion in those declarations is contested, the "signed by counsel with email permission" authentication does not survive serious challenge.

This issue is unlikely to be dispositive on its own but is relevant to any sanctions motion or defamation counter-theory, where the authenticity and personal knowledge of the declarant would be squarely at issue.

Remedies Available to Respondent in Utah

1. Expungement (§ 78B-7-1003): Current Proceeding

The strongest path. Respondent's best argument is equitable: petitioner manufactured the absence of a post-hearing dismissal by walking away before the full hearing. The court has discretion under the "may" language and should exercise it in respondent's favor given the strategic dismissal. Additionally, two witnesses did testify under oath on Sept 22, 2025. Respondent should argue this satisfies the "hearing" requirement of (5)(a)(i), or alternatively that petitioner's failure to appear at the continued hearing satisfies (5)(b).

2. Fee Award (§ 78B-7-701(14))

The civil stalking statute permits fee awards. Respondent's opposition brief explicitly reserved this right: "Christina reserves the right to seek fees under Utah Code § 78B-7-701(14) within the timeframe provided under Utah Rule of Civil Procedure 73(a)." A voluntary dismissal that follows the judge narrowing the order, rejecting the sanctions theory, and approving media coverage suggests the petition lacked the merit to proceed. A fee motion is viable.

3. URCP Rule 11 Sanctions

Rule 11 sanctions require a showing that a filing was frivolous (not warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument for modification) or brought for an improper purpose. The strongest grounds are:

  • The blanket social media ban requested in the petition, which violated Ragsdale II (decided months before the filing).
  • The Sept 17, 2025 sanctions motion premised on social media posts the court then said were never covered by the order.
  • The factual misrepresentation in the expungement opposition that "no evidentiary hearing was held."
  • The Boise incident declaration, which implies Christina orchestrated the restaurant confrontation while omitting that she was in Europe.

Rule 11 motions must follow the safe-harbor procedure: serve the motion on opposing counsel, provide 21 days to withdraw or correct, then file. Given that petitioner dismissed the underlying case, any Rule 11 motion in the original proceeding may be time-barred. The expungement proceeding is a separate procedural vehicle, and respondent should assess with counsel whether Rule 11 applies to filings in that proceeding.

4. Civil Tort: Abuse of Process or Malicious Prosecution

These are the hardest remedies to pursue and require the most research before filing. Key issues:

  • Malicious prosecution requires the underlying proceeding to have terminated in respondent's favor on the merits. Utah courts have not been uniform on whether a voluntary dismissal by the plaintiff constitutes "favorable termination." If the court grants expungement, that may strengthen this argument.
  • Abuse of process does not require favorable termination but requires proof that petitioner used legal process for an improper purpose beyond the ostensible purpose of the proceeding. The strategic dismissal pattern is relevant here, particularly the timing relative to the media coverage approval.
  • These torts carry a statute of limitations. Respondent should not delay consulting counsel on this question.

5. Defamation

Low viability for respondent as a plaintiff. Petitioner's claims about respondent were made primarily in judicial filings (absolutely privileged) or to her own audience about a matter of public record. The more viable defamation theory runs the other way: if petitioner's statements about respondent outside the litigation were materially false and published to third parties, respondent might have a claim. This would require a separate analysis of petitioner's public statements about respondent.

Priority order (1) Win the expungement on equitable and statutory grounds. (2) File for fees under § 78B-7-701(14), already reserved by respondent's counsel. (3) Assess Rule 11 against the expungement opposition's factual misrepresentation about the hearing. (4) Research malicious prosecution / abuse of process with a Utah litigator before the limitations period runs.

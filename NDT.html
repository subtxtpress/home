NDT Pre-Program Crash Course

Before You Start: Get a proper Ishihara color vision test. An optometrist can do it for $20–30, and some Walmart Vision Centers carry it too. Color deficiency is a hard disqualifier for NDT per ASNT guidelines, and the program materials say so outright. Confirm you pass, then work through the five weeks below.

Pace: This syllabus assumes 1–2 hours per day. Compress or stretch as your schedule allows. The order matters more than the speed.

Week 1: Math Foundations

NDT involves real calculation, especially Ultrasonic Testing, where you compute sound velocity, beam angles, and time-of-flight distances. Rusty math will slow you down on day one.

Topics to cover

  • Fractions, decimals, percentages, and unit conversions (inches to mm, for example)
  • Basic algebra: solving for an unknown variable
  • Ratios and proportions
  • Introduction to trigonometry: what sine, cosine, and tangent mean, and how to use them for angle calculations (UT beam angles depend on this)
  • Scientific notation and reading measurement scales

Where to study

  • Khan Academy (khanacademy.org); work through the Algebra Basics and Trigonometry Basics units. Free, self-paced, and you can pick up wherever you left off.
  • Focus on the trig unit circle and the sin/cos/tan relationships. You do not need calculus.

Goal by end of Week 1

Solve a basic angle problem using a sine or cosine formula without needing to look up what those mean.

Week 2: Physics Foundations

Each of the five NDT methods is applied physics. Understanding the underlying principle before class means you are building skill in training, not catching up on concepts.

Sound waves: Ultrasonic Testing

  • What a wave is: frequency, wavelength, amplitude, velocity
  • How sound travels through solid materials vs. liquids vs. air
  • What happens at a material boundary: reflection, refraction, and mode conversion
  • Why flaws reflect sound (impedance mismatch)
  • Search: "ultrasonic testing physics basics"; Olympus NDT's online resources are particularly good here

Electromagnetism: Magnetic Particle Testing

  • What makes a material ferromagnetic (iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys)
  • How a magnetic field is created and what magnetic flux lines look like
  • Why a surface or near-surface crack disrupts a magnetic field (flux leakage)
  • Khan Academy's "Magnetic forces" unit covers this at the right level

Radiation: Radiographic Testing

  • What X-rays and gamma rays are (electromagnetic radiation, very short wavelength)
  • How they penetrate materials and why denser material absorbs more
  • Why a void or crack shows as a dark area on radiographic film
  • Conceptual only; you do not need to know how to operate radiographic equipment yet

Capillary action: Liquid Penetrant Testing

  • Why liquid is drawn into tight cracks by surface tension
  • How a developer draws the penetrant back out and makes it visible
  • This one is intuitive once you look it up; 20 minutes is enough

Light and optics: Visual Testing

  • How lighting angle affects the visibility of surface features
  • Contrast: why a flaw visible under one light is invisible under another
  • VT is deceptively technical; it is not just looking at something

Goal by end of Week 2

You can explain in plain language what physical principle each of the five NDT methods relies on, and why a flaw in a material would be detectable by each method.

Week 3: Materials Science and Weld Defects

NDT exists to find defects before they cause failures. Understanding what defects look like, why they form, and why they matter in submarine manufacturing gives everything else context.

What to learn

  • Common weld defects and what causes each one:
    • Porosity (gas trapped in weld metal)
    • Cracks (solidification cracks, hydrogen-induced cracks)
    • Lack of fusion (weld metal didn't bond to the base metal)
    • Lack of penetration (weld didn't reach the root)
    • Undercut, overlap, inclusions
  • Why these matter in a pressure vessel or submarine hull; the stakes of a missed defect
  • Basic metals: what steel is, what stainless steel is, why different alloys behave differently under inspection
  • What a heat-affected zone (HAZ) is and why it is often where defects hide

Where to study

  • TWI (The Welding Institute) at twi-global.com; search "weld defects" in their Knowledge section. Free articles written for practitioners, not academics.
  • Lincoln Electric's Welding Academy (lincolnelectric.com/en/education-center) covers defect types clearly with visuals.
  • Search YouTube for "weld defect types explained"; there are several 10–15 minute videos that cover the visuals better than text can.

Goal by end of Week 3

You can name the six most common weld defects, describe what causes each, and explain why each one is dangerous in a submarine hull component.

Week 4: Technical Drawing Basics

NDT technicians work from engineering drawings to know where to inspect, what method to use, and what acceptance criteria apply. You do not need to read complex design drawings; you need the basics.

Topics to cover

  • Orthographic projection: how a 3D object is shown in 2D from multiple views (front, top, side)
  • How to read dimensions and units on a drawing
  • What tolerance callouts mean (±0.005", for example) and why they matter in precision manufacturing
  • What weld symbols look like on a drawing and what they indicate
  • How to find a revision block and understand that drawings are versioned documents
  • What a section view is and how to read it

Where to study

  • YouTube: search "how to read engineering drawings for beginners"; pick any result with 100k+ views, they are mostly solid
  • GD&T basics: you do not need to be fluent, but knowing what a flatness or concentricity callout is will help
  • Search: "weld symbols explained engineering drawings"

Goal by end of Week 4

Given a simple part drawing, you can identify where a weld is located, what the tolerance is, and understand what a section view is showing you.

Week 5: NDT Methods and Industry Orientation

Now that you have the foundations, spend the final week orienting yourself to the industry, the credentialing structure, and getting a preview of actual NDT procedures.

The ASNT credentialing ladder

  • Go to asnt.org and read through their overview of SNT-TC-1A (the recommended practice that governs most employer-based certification programs) and CP-189 (the standard for ASNT's own certification program)
  • Understand the three levels: Level I (performs tests under supervision), Level II (interprets results, writes procedures, supervises Level I), Level III (develops programs, trains and certifies others, approves procedures)
  • The ATDM program positions you for Level I and II classroom hours. Level III is years of work and testing away, but knowing the ladder helps you understand where you are entering

Preview each NDT method

  • Olympus NDT encyclopedia (olympus-ims.com/en/ndt-tutorials/): read the intro pages for UT, MT, PT, RT, and VT. These are written for practitioners and will feel relevant, not abstract, after four weeks of prep.
  • Search YouTube for short demos of each method; watching someone actually run a UT scan or do a PT inspection makes the classroom procedures land immediately

NAVSEA context

  • The ATDM program is specifically calibrated to NAVSEA (Naval Sea Systems Command) NDT requirements. Search "NAVSEA T9074-AS-GIB-010/271"; this is the document that governs NDT on submarine components. You do not need to read it, but knowing it exists and what it covers will make you sound like you did your homework.
  • Buildsubmarines.com is worth browsing; it gives context on the defense industrial base you are preparing to enter.

Goal by end of Week 5

You can explain the ASNT Level I/II/III ladder, name the five NDT methods and their physical principles, and describe the difference between a surface flaw and a subsurface flaw and which methods find each.

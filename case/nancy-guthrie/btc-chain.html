About this transaction: Under the hood, Bitcoin requires every transaction to account for 100% of the sending wallet's balance — any amount not sent to the intended recipient must be explicitly directed somewhere in the same transaction. Wallet apps hide this artifact from users, but it's visible on the blockchain. The FBI's sending wallet held ~$300; they sent $152 to the ransom address, and the remaining $148 was automatically routed to a second address by their wallet software — the FBI almost certainly did not specify that address manually. If they used a custodial exchange (Coinbase, Kraken, etc.), the exchange manages that second address on their behalf and folds the balance back into their account — they would never see a separate address at all. If they used non-custodial wallet software, the software auto-generated the address and the FBI retains access through their wallet. Which type the FBI used is unconfirmed. Why their sending wallet held more than the $152 they intended to send is also unknown — as is why they chose $152 specifically. Both figures are millions less than the $4 million demanded by the kidnappers as well, as the sole Bitcoin demanded by the informant.

OP_RETURN Deadline Search

The theory: The Feb. 10 message ("We are ready to pay") arrived after both ransom deadlines, paired with a wallet that was never touched. That pattern fits an informant channel, not direct contact with the actual kidnappers. The question was whether the FBI sent a separate OP_RETURN message near either deadline — Feb. 5 at 5:00 PM or Feb. 9 at 2:00 PM Tucson time — to reach the actual kidnappers. Every transaction in blocks around those two deadlines was scanned.
